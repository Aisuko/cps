Circuit-Level Preference Steering under Knowledge Conflict in Large Language Models

When what a model reads disagrees with what it remembers, CPS lets you decide which one wins — by turning the right attention heads up and the competing ones down. No training required.

01 · Summary

One idea: steer knowledge preference through coordinated attention-head circuits

Large language models carry parametric knowledge learned in pre-training and receive contextual knowledge at inference time (retrieval, in-context examples). When the two conflict, behaviour becomes unpredictable — it varies with model scale, training frequency and the strength of the evidence.

Existing control happens at the prompt, training or output-distribution level. Mechanistic work finds specialised attention heads, but usually studies heads one at a time, and per-head interventions get expensive as candidates grow.

Circuit Preference Steering (CPS) jointly characterises head roles under two contrasting, externally specified preference conditions — favour the context, or favour parametric memory. By looking at how each head’s role transitions between the conditions, CPS identifies a contextual circuit Cc and a parametric circuit Cp, then scales their outputs at inference: lightweight, training-free and bidirectional.

CPS does not decide which source is more trustworthy. The preference is specified externally (e.g. “prefer the verified database”, “down-weight unreliable retrieved text”); CPS executes it.

02 · The problem

A controlled conflict: same evidence, two different goals

Each instance is a tuple (question, org_context, counter_context, org_answer, counter_answer). Using the counter_context as input creates a conflict: the evidence contradicts what the model memorised.

  • Context-Preference (Cont.) — the desired prediction follows the contextual information.
  • Parametric-Preference (Para.) — the desired prediction follows parametric knowledge.

Both conditions share identical contextual evidence; only the target answer differs. That isolates each knowledge source’s contribution and makes head roles directly comparable. Flip the switch to see it →

03 · Method

Setup stage → Inference stage

  1. Head role identification

    Causal Head Gating (CHG) jointly learns a gate gℓ,h ∈ [0,1] for every head, modulating its output o → g·o under a next-token loss with a regulariser λ·Σσ⁻¹(g). Retention (λ>0) and suppression (λ<0) give gates G⁺ and G⁻, yielding roles Fac (active under suppressive pressure), Inter (removed despite retention) and Irr (negligible). CPS runs this under both preference conditions.

  2. Role-transition analysis

    A head that looks facilitating in one condition is ambiguous alone. Comparing roles across conditions resolves it: heads that facilitate one preference but are interfering or irrelevant under the other are functionally specialised. Interfering and irrelevant are grouped because neither supports the preference.

  3. Circuit construction

    Cp = { r(Para.)=Fac ∧ r(Cont.)∈{Inter,Irr} } and Cc = { r(Cont.)=Fac ∧ r(Para.)∈{Inter,Irr} }. Members are ranked by their gate g⁻ and the top-K form Sc(K) and Sp(K). Masking each circuit tests whether it is functionally associated with its knowledge source.

  4. Generation control

    Head outputs are scaled by s = 1+α for Cc, 1+β for Cp and 1 otherwise. With strength δ>0: Context policy (α,β) = (+δ, −δ/(1+δ)); Parametric policy (α,β) = (−δ/(1+δ), +δ). No parameter updates, no extra pass.

05 · Case study

Redirecting a model from memory to evidence

Gemma-2-9B on NQ-Swap under the Context-Preference policy (K = 8 heads per circuit). The context states a counterfactual fact; Vanilla often answers from memory. CPS shifts the answer to the one supported by the context — while leaving already-aligned answers intact.

06 · Results

Evidence across models, benchmarks and baselines

Models

Llama-3.1-8B, Mistral-7B-v0.1, Gemma-2-9B

Datasets

ConflictBank, ConflictQA, NQ-Swap, PopQA-Swap; unseen: DynamicQA, WikiContradict

Baselines

Vanilla · Random-K · Prompt · PH3 · JuICE

Metric

Exact Match against the target answer of each condition; mean ± std over seeds 0, 1, 2

Intervention-based circuit validation

If the circuits really are tied to different knowledge sources, silencing them should move preference in opposite directions.

Context steering works best

CPS gives the strongest mean EM across most model–dataset pairs, e.g. Llama-3.1-8B on NQ-Swap 0.189 → 0.324.

Parametric steering is harder

Gains are smaller — models are susceptible to contextual influence — but CPS is best or competitive, e.g. NQ-Swap 0.111 → 0.200 on Llama.

Both operations matter

Boosting the target circuit and suppressing the competing one beats either alone.

It transfers

Circuits localised on NQ-Swap lift performance on unseen DynamicQA and WikiContradict without re-localisation.

07 · Scope & ethics

What the paper does — and does not — claim

Limitations the authors state

  • Three runs per setting: results are descriptive comparisons, not evidence of statistical significance; some gains are small relative to run-to-run variation.
  • Targeted masking provides functional evidence on Gemma-2-9B only; extending it to other models, and studying interactions and pathways within the head groups, is future work.
  • Baselines focus on attention-head interventions; comparison with broader activation-steering and context-aware decoding methods is out of scope.
  • The ablation uses a subset of the test set due to limited compute.

Ethical considerations

The work uses public benchmarks and open-weight models, with no personal data. Steering a model toward context or memory has dual-use risk: a poor policy could promote misleading evidence or inaccurate memory. CPS executes a specified preference — it is not a substitute for source verification or factuality checks. In high-stakes domains (healthcare, law, finance) it should be paired with reliable evidence validation, transparent intervention policies and human oversight.
